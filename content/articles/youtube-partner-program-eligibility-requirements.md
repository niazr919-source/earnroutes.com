The YouTube Partner Program is the mechanism that turns views into money. Most guides quote the subscriber threshold and stop there, which leaves creators surprised twice: first by the second qualifying route, and later by the policies that decide whether a channel that *did* qualify actually gets approved.

Here is what YouTube states, and what each requirement means in practice.

## The Two Routes to the Threshold

You need **one** of these, not both:

- **The long-form route:** 1,000 subscribers with **4,000 qualified public watch hours** in the last 12 months.
- **The Shorts route:** 1,000 subscribers with **10 million qualified Shorts views** in the last 90 days.

Both routes require 1,000 subscribers. What differs is how you demonstrate viewership.

:::info
Those two numbers are not equivalent in difficulty, and the gap surprises people. 4,000 watch hours is 240,000 minutes of viewing. Ten million Shorts views in 90 days is a genuinely large audience. For most creators, the long-form route is far more achievable — a channel with 40 videos averaging 100 watch hours each is there.
:::

The watch-hour clock is a rolling 12-month window, and the Shorts clock a rolling 90 days. Hours earned 13 months ago no longer count, which means a channel that stalls can fall back below the threshold.

## The Requirements Nobody Mentions

Beyond the numbers, YouTube requires that you:

- **Comply with the channel monetization policies** (covered in detail below — this is where applications actually fail).
- **Live in a country or region where YPP is available.**
- **Have no active Community Guidelines strikes** on the channel.
- **Enable 2-Step Verification** on the Google Account.
- **Have access to advanced features.**
- **Link or create an AdSense for YouTube account.**

:::warning
Two of these block applications quietly. An active Community Guidelines strike makes you ineligible until it expires — strikes last 90 days — so a single strike can delay monetization by a full quarter. And advanced features require identity verification, which can take days. Sort both out before you hit the threshold, not after.
:::

## Where Applications Actually Fail: The Monetization Policies

Hitting 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 hours gets you reviewed. It doesn't get you approved. Review is where YouTube checks your channel against its monetization policies, and this is where AI-assisted and automation-heavy channels run into trouble.

YouTube's standard is that content must be **"original and authentic"** and must not be **"mass-produced, generic, repetitive, or manipulative."** Content should be made for viewer enjoyment rather than, in YouTube's words, "solely for the purpose of getting views."

### Inauthentic and repetitious content

Videos shouldn't feel template-based or interchangeable. YouTube's requirement is that the substance of each video be **"materially varied and deliver creative, educational, or other value."**

The policy explicitly addresses AI-generated content, prohibiting the use of **"generic or unoriginal templates"** without an authentic creator perspective. This is the single most important sentence for anyone building an automated channel: using AI tools is not itself disqualifying, but producing interchangeable videos from a fixed template is.

:::warning
This is the trap in most "faceless YouTube automation" advice. A pipeline that generates 50 videos from one script template, one voice, and one stock-footage pattern is precisely the pattern the policy names. The AI isn't the problem — the sameness is.
:::

### Reused content

Channels cannot simply repackage existing content without significant addition. YouTube distinguishes clearly:

**Acceptable:** reaction videos with meaningful commentary, critical reviews, edited footage with substantive modification.

**Not acceptable:** unedited compilations, minimal-change reposts, other people's content with a caption bolted on.

The test is whether *you* added something a viewer couldn't get from the original.

### Community Guidelines and advertiser-friendly content

Two separate bars. Community Guidelines decide whether content stays on YouTube at all — as YouTube puts it, "Content that violates YouTube's Community Guidelines is not eligible for monetization and will be removed from YouTube." The advertiser-friendly guidelines are stricter and decide whether a video that's allowed to exist can carry ads. Topics like violence, profanity, and controversial subjects can leave a video live but demonetized or limited.

### Creator responsibility

Creators must not artificially inflate engagement metrics or engage in deceptive practices. Buying subscribers or views is a fast route to disqualification, and purchased engagement is easy to detect because it doesn't behave like real viewership.

## What Happens If You Break the Rules Later

Approval isn't permanent. Violations can result in limited ad earnings on specific videos, suspension of monetization, or channel termination in serious cases.

The realistic risk for most channels isn't termination — it's a slow drift into "limited or no ads" on individual videos, which shows up as revenue quietly falling while views hold steady. If that happens, check the advertiser-friendly guidelines against your recent topics before assuming the algorithm changed.

## A Realistic Path to the Threshold

The numbers are achievable but not quick. A workable approach:

1. **Pick a subject you can make 100 genuinely different videos about.** If you can only think of 10 angles, the repetition policy will catch you by video 30.
2. **Publish consistently rather than in bursts.** The watch-hour window is rolling; steady output keeps the total climbing.
3. **Optimise for watch time, not view count,** on the long-form route. Twenty minutes watched from one viewer beats twenty one-minute views.
4. **Sort out verification and 2-Step early** so nothing blocks you at the threshold.
5. **Read the monetization policies before you build a production pipeline,** not after. Retrofitting variety into 50 template videos is much harder than designing for it.

:::action
If you are building an AI-assisted channel, write down what makes each video materially different from the last — a distinct argument, original research, your own analysis, genuinely varied structure. If you can't answer that for a given video, it's the kind of content the policy is written to exclude.
:::
