If you've read anything about Kindle pricing, you've been told the magic number is **$9.99** — price above it and your royalty halves.

That stopped being true on **7 July 2026**. The 70% royalty band on Amazon.com now runs **$2.99 to $12.99**. The $9.99 ceiling had been in place since **2007**, so nearly every guide, course and forum post written before mid-2026 is now giving outdated pricing advice.

Here's how the royalty maths actually works.

## The Two Options

You choose one per book.

### The 35% option

The simple one. You receive **35% of your list price excluding VAT**, with no delivery cost deduction:

> Royalty Rate × (List Price − VAT) = Royalty

A $0.99 book with no VAT earns **$0.35** per sale. The same book sold where 20% VAT applies earns about **$0.29**, because VAT reduces the base the royalty is calculated on.

### The 70% option

Higher, with conditions:

> Royalty Rate × (List Price − VAT − Delivery Costs) = Royalty

You receive **70% in Amazon's designated 70% territories**, and **35% for sales outside them**. Note what determines this: your **customer's** location, not yours. You do not need to live in a 70% territory to earn the higher rate.

:::info
Because the split is by buyer location, a single book earns both rates simultaneously. Your KDP reports show a blend, which is why the average per-sale figure rarely matches a clean 70% calculation.
:::

## Delivery Costs: The Deduction Most Guides Skip

The 70% option deducts a **delivery cost** before calculating your royalty. It's based on file size, and Amazon states it **averages about $0.06 per unit**.

This matters more than $0.06 suggests, because it scales with your file. Amazon's own example charges **€0.10 per MB** in Spain — so a 1 MB file costs €0.10, and a 20 MB illustrated book costs €2.00 per sale.

**The 35% option has no delivery cost deduction at all.** For a large, image-heavy book this can flip the comparison entirely.

:::tip
Before uploading, compress your images. Delivery cost is charged on every single sale forever, so shaving a 15 MB file to 4 MB is one of the few optimisations in self-publishing that pays out permanently.
:::

## What the $12.99 Change Means

At the old $9.99 ceiling, a book priced at $10.99 dropped to 35% — earning **$3.85** instead of the roughly **$7.65** it would have earned at 70%. Pricing above $9.99 was close to irrational, which is why so much of the market clustered at $9.99 exactly.

That constraint is gone up to $12.99. A $12.99 book at 70%, less an average $0.06 delivery cost, earns about **$9.05** per sale versus **$4.55** at 35%.

Whether readers in your category will *pay* $12.99 is a separate question that pricing bands don't answer. But the penalty that made it pointless to try no longer applies below $12.99.

| $14.99 | $5.25 | — | No, above $12.99 |

## The Conditions That Catch People

**Public domain works only qualify for 35%.** If you're republishing out-of-copyright material, the 70% option is unavailable regardless of price.

**Price matching cuts both ways.** If Amazon discounts your book to match a competitor, you receive 70% of the **sale price**, not your list price, in eligible territories. Listing the same book more cheaply elsewhere can therefore reduce your Amazon royalty.

**The 35% option runs to $200.** Its band is much wider — from $0.99 (the minimum varies with file size) up to $200 — which is why textbooks and technical manuals often sit at 35% by necessity.

**VAT reduces your base everywhere it applies.** Your royalty is calculated after VAT is removed, so identical list prices produce different royalties in different markets.

:::action
If you published before July 2026 and priced at $9.99 because of the old ceiling, that constraint no longer binds below $12.99. Revisit it — but test rather than assuming, since a higher price only helps if the sales volume holds.
:::
