"Low value content" is the most frustrating rejection AdSense sends, because it names no page, no policy, and no fix. You're told the site isn't good enough and left to guess.

The message is vague, but the standard behind it isn't. Google publishes, in detail, what it considers valuable content — and that documentation is the closest thing to a rubric you'll get.

:::warning
Almost every article ranking for this problem quotes a statistic like "65% of rejections were fixed by removing thin content." No such data exists. Google publishes no rejection statistics, and nobody outside Google can measure this. Treat every number you see on this topic — including recommended word counts — as invented unless it links to a Google page.
:::

## Start Here: AdSense's Actual Requirement

The eligibility page is short. It asks for content that is "high-quality, original, and attract an audience," plus policy compliance and an applicant aged 18 or over.

That's the whole content bar. There's no word count, no page count, no traffic minimum. So when a reviewer decides your site has "low value," they're applying Google's broader definition of helpful content — which is documented properly.

## The Questions Google Tells You to Ask

Google publishes a self-assessment for content quality. Read these as the rubric, because functionally that's what they are:

- **"Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?"**
- **"Does the content provide a substantial, complete, or comprehensive description of the topic?"**
- **"Does the content provide insightful analysis or interesting information that is beyond the obvious?"**
- Does it avoid exaggerated or shocking headlines, and is it well-produced without spelling or style problems?
- **"Is this the sort of page you'd want to bookmark, share with a friend, or recommend?"**

Now apply them honestly to your own pages. Most rejected sites fail on the first and third: the content is accurate and readable, but it's a restatement of what ten other pages already say. Being correct is not the same as being valuable.

:::info
The bookmark question is the most useful one. Take your five best articles and ask whether a stranger would genuinely save them. If the honest answer is no, adding ten more of the same won't change the outcome.
:::

## Google's Expertise Criteria

Separately, Google asks whether content demonstrates:

- Clear authorship — bylines and background information about who wrote it
- Evidence of real-world expertise, such as having actually used the products or visited the places described
- Factual accuracy with no easily-verifiable errors
- Recognition as a trusted authority on the topic

Google's framework here is **E-E-A-T** — Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness — and it states plainly: **"Of these aspects, trust is most important."**

For money, health, or safety topics — the YMYL category, which "how to earn online" content sits squarely inside — Google weights all of this more heavily. If your site is about income, you are held to a higher standard than a site about houseplants.

:::warning
A specific trap: inventing expert authors. Fabricated bylines with impressive-sounding credentials are a direct hit on the "trust" element Google calls most important, and they're easy to check. A single honest editorial byline beats four invented experts.
:::

## What Google Says Not to Do

The helpful-content guidance explicitly warns against content made "primarily to attract visits from search engines," and names:

- Producing numerous articles on varied topics hoping some rank
- Using "extensive automation to produce content on many different topics"
- Changing publication dates to look fresh without substantially updating anything
- Writing about topics only because they're trending, rather than for your actual audience

The spam policies go further, covering **scaled content abuse** — mass-producing pages primarily to rank, with or without AI — and **thin affiliate pages** that add nothing to the merchant's own description.

:::info
AI assistance is not itself disqualifying. Google's objection is to content produced at scale that adds nothing, regardless of how it was written. An AI-assisted article containing original analysis, real testing, and specific detail is fine. Fifty near-identical AI articles are the exact pattern the policy targets.
:::

## The Fix Most People Skip: Deleting

Publishers respond to this rejection by writing more. Frequently the correct move is the opposite.

If your site has 30 pages and 20 are thin, the reviewer's impression is formed by the whole site, not your best work. Removing weak pages raises the average immediately, and costs nothing you'll miss.

Work through every page and sort it into three piles:

1. **Genuinely useful** — original angle, specific detail, answers the question fully. Keep.
2. **Fixable** — right topic, shallow execution. Rewrite with real specifics, examples, and sources.
3. **Filler** — written to have a post that day, or to target a keyword. Delete or set to draft.

Be ruthless with pile three. A site of 12 strong articles reviews far better than 30 mixed ones.

## Non-Content Problems That Trigger the Same Message

A reviewer sees the whole site, and these get lumped into the same rejection:

- **Missing or incomplete policy pages.** Privacy policy, terms, contact, and a real About page — all reachable from every page.
- **A vague About page.** State who runs the site and why they're worth reading. This directly addresses Google's authorship criterion.
- **Broken navigation or dead links.**
- **Placeholder content** — "Lorem ipsum", "Coming soon", empty category pages.
- **Ad placeholders left in the layout.** Empty boxes labelled "ad here" read as an unfinished site.
- **No original images**, only stock photos every competing site also uses.

## Before You Reapply

The reviewer sees your site as it is when they look, so give the changes time to be live and crawled. Reapplying the same afternoon means the review may still hit cached or unchanged pages.

A practical sequence:

1. Delete or draft every thin page.
2. Rewrite the salvageable ones with genuine specifics — real numbers, real sources, real experience.
3. Make sure every claim you can cite links to a primary source.
4. Complete your About, contact, privacy, and terms pages.
5. Publish two or three genuinely strong new pieces.
6. Wait a couple of weeks so Google recrawls, then reapply.

:::action
Before reapplying, open your five best pages and answer Google's own question for each: does this provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis? If you can't point to the specific original element in a given page, that page isn't ready — and neither is the site.
:::
