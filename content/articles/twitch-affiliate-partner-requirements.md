Twitch has two monetization tiers, and the distance between them is much larger than most people expect. One is reachable in a fortnight. The other requires a genuine audience.

## Affiliate: Four Requirements, All Small

Twitch states you must meet **four** requirements:

| Requirement | Threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Followers | **25** |
| Total streamed | **4 hours** |
| Different days streamed | **4** |
| Average viewers | **3** (across those 4 different days) |

You must also enable **Two-Factor Authentication** before onboarding can begin — Twitch requires it for all Affiliates and Partners.

:::info
An average of **three** viewers. Not three hundred. This is the genuinely accessible tier, and it is why "become a Twitch Affiliate" is achievable for almost anyone who streams consistently for a couple of weeks and tells a few friends.
:::

Twitch also notes that streamers aged 13–17 may join with parental or guardian consent and supervision.

## Partner: A Different Order of Magnitude

Twitch's Partnership criteria:

- Complete the **Path to Partner** achievement
- For the **two consecutive months** before applying, have at least **6 individual streams** meeting a minimum of **75 average viewers per 30-day period**
- Broadcast content conforming to the **Community Guidelines, Terms of Service and DMCA Guidelines**
- Twitch also **considers suspension history** when evaluating applications

Twitch adds that it may accept applications outside these criteria in certain circumstances, including exceptional viewership growth.

:::warning
Note the jump: **3 average viewers** for Affiliate, **75 average viewers** for Partner — twenty-five times more, sustained over two consecutive months rather than four days. Anyone treating Affiliate as "nearly Partner" has badly misjudged the gap.
:::

One detail worth knowing before you plan around it: Twitch states that viewership from **front-page promotions or embeds does not count** toward viewership requirement calculations. A burst of borrowed attention will not carry you over the line.

## What Each Tier Actually Pays

**Bits.** Twitch states it provides participating Partners a share of Bits revenue **equal to 1 cent per Bit** used to Cheer for them. So a viewer cheering 500 Bits is worth about $5 to the streamer.

**Subscriptions.** Viewers subscribe at Tier 1, Tier 2, Tier 3, or free via Prime. Twitch notes that revenue from a subscription **may vary depending on the country the subscriber lives in**, since local subscription prices differ.

**Ads.** Partners earn a share of revenue from ads on their channel.

**Emotes.** Partners can unlock up to **60** channel emotes — not revenue directly, but a real retention tool.

:::info
Twitch does not state a single standard subscription split on its Partner overview page. What it does publish is the **Plus Program**, offering two levels of *enhanced* subscription net revenue share for qualifying Affiliates and Partners: **Level 1 at 60/40** and **Level 2 at 70/30**. Those being described as enhanced tells you the default sits below them.
:::

## How This Compares to YouTube

Worth setting side by side, because creators often choose between them:

| | Entry requirement | Creator share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Twitch Affiliate** | 25 followers, 3 average viewers | Bits at 1 cent each; subscription share |
| **YouTube Partner Program** | 1,000 subscribers + 4,000 watch hours (or 10M Shorts views) | 55% of ad revenue, 70% of memberships and Super Thanks |

Twitch's entry bar is dramatically lower. YouTube's is higher but its published shares are clearer, and its content keeps earning long after upload — where a stream's value is mostly consumed live.

:::tip
The two aren't mutually exclusive, and the sensible pattern is obvious once you see the thresholds: stream on Twitch where the monetization bar is low, and publish the edited highlights to YouTube where the content keeps working. One session, two channels, two revenue models.
:::

## A Realistic Reading

**Affiliate is a milestone, not an income.** Three average viewers cheering occasional Bits is not a living. What it does is switch monetization on, which matters because you cannot build subscriber habits on a channel that has no subscribe button.

**Partner is where the economics change** — and 75 average viewers sustained for two months is a real audience, typically representing many months of consistent streaming.

**Live is unforgiving.** Unlike a blog post or a YouTube video, a stream earns while it's happening and very little afterwards. That makes consistency of schedule matter far more than it does in other online income routes.

**Income is taxable.** Bits, subs and ad revenue are self-employment income. In the US, self-employment tax alone is 15.3% of net earnings before income tax applies.

:::action
Check your Achievements Dashboard on Twitch rather than guessing where you stand — it tracks progress against all four Affiliate requirements directly. If you are close, the missing one is usually the 3-viewer average, which is a scheduling problem more than an audience problem.
:::
