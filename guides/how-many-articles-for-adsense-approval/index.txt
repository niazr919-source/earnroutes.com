<p>Here is the actual answer: <strong>Google publishes no minimum.</strong> Not on the eligibility page, not in the Program policies, not anywhere in its documentation. Every specific number you have read on this topic was made up by the person who wrote it.</p>
<p>That's not a technicality. It changes what you should be doing.</p>
<h2 id="what-the-eligibility-page-actually-requires">What the Eligibility Page Actually Requires</h2>
<p>Google's stated requirements are short:</p>
<ol>
<li>Your own <strong>unique and interesting content</strong>, which is "high-quality, original, and attract an audience"</li>
<li>Compliance with the <strong>Program policies</strong></li>
<li>Being at least <strong>18 years of age</strong></li>
</ol>
<p>No article count. No word count. No traffic threshold. No site age.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>This is genuinely useful information, not a dodge. If you've been holding off applying until you hit an imaginary 30-article target, you may be waiting for a milestone that doesn't exist — while a real blocker like a missing privacy policy goes unfixed.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="why-everyone-invents-a-number-anyway">Why Everyone Invents a Number Anyway</h2>
<p>Because "how many articles" is the wrong question, and a number feels more actionable than "make it good."</p>
<p>Article count is a proxy for the thing Google actually assesses: whether the site looks like a real publication with something to offer. Twenty-five thin posts don't achieve that. Eight excellent ones might.</p>
<p>Google's helpful-content guidance asks whether content provides "original information, reporting, research, or analysis" and whether it's "the sort of page you'd want to bookmark, share with a friend, or recommend." Neither question has a quantity component.</p>
<p>More pointedly, the spam policies name <strong>scaled content abuse</strong> — mass-producing pages primarily to rank rather than to help anyone. Racing to an article count is precisely the behaviour that produces it. Chasing the number can actively cause the rejection you were trying to avoid.</p>
<h2 id="what-actually-decides-it">What Actually Decides It</h2>
<p>A reviewer forms an impression of the whole site. What moves that impression:</p>
<p><strong>Does the site have a clear purpose?</strong> A reviewer should understand within seconds what it's about and who it's for. A blog covering crypto, recipes, and travel insurance reads as a keyword farm regardless of length.</p>
<p><strong>Is the content demonstrably original?</strong> Not merely reworded from what already ranks. Original means your own analysis, your own testing, your own data, or primary sources nobody else bothered to read.</p>
<p><strong>Do the required pages exist and work?</strong> Privacy policy, terms, contact, and a substantive About page, reachable from every page. This is the most common non-content blocker and takes an afternoon.</p>
<p><strong>Does navigation work?</strong> Categories that lead somewhere, no dead links, no empty archive pages.</p>
<p><strong>Is the site finished?</strong> No placeholder text, no "coming soon", no empty ad boxes.</p>
<p><strong>Does anything look deceptive?</strong> Invented author credentials, fake testimonials, or copied content will sink an otherwise decent site — Google's E-E-A-T framework states that of Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trustworthiness, <strong>"trust is most important."</strong></p>
<h2 id="a-better-question-to-ask">A Better Question to Ask</h2>
<p>Instead of "how many articles do I need," ask:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>If a stranger landed on my site, would they believe a real person built this to help them — or would they see something assembled to hold ads?</p>
</blockquote>
<p>That's what a reviewer decides. Every genuine improvement follows from answering it honestly.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-tip">
<p class="callout-label">💡 Pro Tip</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>A practical test: pick your three strongest articles and imagine sending them to someone who actually has the problem they solve. If you'd feel confident doing that, you're closer than an article count suggests. If you'd hesitate, no quantity of additional posts will help.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="so-what-should-you-do">So What Should You Do?</h2>
<p>A defensible approach, without pretending it's a rule:</p>
<p><strong>Publish until the site is genuinely useful to someone.</strong> For most niches that lands somewhere in the low-to-mid tens of articles — not because Google says so, but because it usually takes that many to cover a topic properly and look like a real publication rather than a test site.</p>
<p><strong>Cover one subject properly</strong> rather than scattering across unrelated topics. Depth reads as expertise; breadth without depth reads as a content farm.</p>
<p><strong>Write each piece as if the count didn't exist.</strong> If you're padding to reach a number, you're producing exactly the filler that causes rejections.</p>
<p><strong>Fix the boring things first.</strong> Policy pages, contact page, an About page that says who you are. These are cheap, mandatory in practice, and block more applications than article count ever has.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-action">
<p class="callout-label">✅ Action Step</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Before applying, do this instead of counting: open every page on your site and ask whether you'd keep it if there were no AdSense application. Delete the ones that fail. A smaller site of genuinely useful pages reviews better than a padded one — and the deleting takes an hour, not a month.</p>
</div>
