c:T2018,<p>The YouTube Partner Program is the mechanism that turns views into money. Most guides quote the subscriber threshold and stop there, which leaves creators surprised twice: first by the second qualifying route, and later by the policies that decide whether a channel that <em>did</em> qualify actually gets approved.</p>
<p>Here is what YouTube states, and what each requirement means in practice.</p>
<h2 id="the-two-routes-to-the-threshold">The Two Routes to the Threshold</h2>
<p>You need <strong>one</strong> of these, not both:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The long-form route:</strong> 1,000 subscribers with <strong>4,000 qualified public watch hours</strong> in the last 12 months.</li>
<li><strong>The Shorts route:</strong> 1,000 subscribers with <strong>10 million qualified Shorts views</strong> in the last 90 days.</li>
</ul>
<p>Both routes require 1,000 subscribers. What differs is how you demonstrate viewership.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Those two numbers are not equivalent in difficulty, and the gap surprises people. 4,000 watch hours is 240,000 minutes of viewing. Ten million Shorts views in 90 days is a genuinely large audience. For most creators, the long-form route is far more achievable — a channel with 40 videos averaging 100 watch hours each is there.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>The watch-hour clock is a rolling 12-month window, and the Shorts clock a rolling 90 days. Hours earned 13 months ago no longer count, which means a channel that stalls can fall back below the threshold.</p>
<h2 id="the-requirements-nobody-mentions">The Requirements Nobody Mentions</h2>
<p>Beyond the numbers, YouTube requires that you:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Comply with the channel monetization policies</strong> (covered in detail below — this is where applications actually fail).</li>
<li><strong>Live in a country or region where YPP is available.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Have no active Community Guidelines strikes</strong> on the channel.</li>
<li><strong>Enable 2-Step Verification</strong> on the Google Account.</li>
<li><strong>Have access to advanced features.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Link or create an AdSense for YouTube account.</strong></li>
</ul>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-warning">
<p class="callout-label">⚠️ Warning</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Two of these block applications quietly. An active Community Guidelines strike makes you ineligible until it expires — strikes last 90 days — so a single strike can delay monetization by a full quarter. And advanced features require identity verification, which can take days. Sort both out before you hit the threshold, not after.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="where-applications-actually-fail-the-monetization-policies">Where Applications Actually Fail: The Monetization Policies</h2>
<p>Hitting 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 hours gets you reviewed. It doesn't get you approved. Review is where YouTube checks your channel against its monetization policies, and this is where AI-assisted and automation-heavy channels run into trouble.</p>
<p>YouTube's standard is that content must be <strong>"original and authentic"</strong> and must not be <strong>"mass-produced, generic, repetitive, or manipulative."</strong> Content should be made for viewer enjoyment rather than, in YouTube's words, "solely for the purpose of getting views."</p>
<h3 id="inauthentic-and-repetitious-content">Inauthentic and repetitious content</h3>
<p>Videos shouldn't feel template-based or interchangeable. YouTube's requirement is that the substance of each video be <strong>"materially varied and deliver creative, educational, or other value."</strong></p>
<p>The policy explicitly addresses AI-generated content, prohibiting the use of <strong>"generic or unoriginal templates"</strong> without an authentic creator perspective. This is the single most important sentence for anyone building an automated channel: using AI tools is not itself disqualifying, but producing interchangeable videos from a fixed template is.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-warning">
<p class="callout-label">⚠️ Warning</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>This is the trap in most "faceless YouTube automation" advice. A pipeline that generates 50 videos from one script template, one voice, and one stock-footage pattern is precisely the pattern the policy names. The AI isn't the problem — the sameness is.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h3 id="reused-content">Reused content</h3>
<p>Channels cannot simply repackage existing content without significant addition. YouTube distinguishes clearly:</p>
<p><strong>Acceptable:</strong> reaction videos with meaningful commentary, critical reviews, edited footage with substantive modification.</p>
<p><strong>Not acceptable:</strong> unedited compilations, minimal-change reposts, other people's content with a caption bolted on.</p>
<p>The test is whether <em>you</em> added something a viewer couldn't get from the original.</p>
<h3 id="community-guidelines-and-advertiser-friendly-content">Community Guidelines and advertiser-friendly content</h3>
<p>Two separate bars. Community Guidelines decide whether content stays on YouTube at all — as YouTube puts it, "Content that violates YouTube's Community Guidelines is not eligible for monetization and will be removed from YouTube." The advertiser-friendly guidelines are stricter and decide whether a video that's allowed to exist can carry ads. Topics like violence, profanity, and controversial subjects can leave a video live but demonetized or limited.</p>
<h3 id="creator-responsibility">Creator responsibility</h3>
<p>Creators must not artificially inflate engagement metrics or engage in deceptive practices. Buying subscribers or views is a fast route to disqualification, and purchased engagement is easy to detect because it doesn't behave like real viewership.</p>
<h2 id="what-happens-if-you-break-the-rules-later">What Happens If You Break the Rules Later</h2>
<p>Approval isn't permanent. Violations can result in limited ad earnings on specific videos, suspension of monetization, or channel termination in serious cases.</p>
<p>The realistic risk for most channels isn't termination — it's a slow drift into "limited or no ads" on individual videos, which shows up as revenue quietly falling while views hold steady. If that happens, check the advertiser-friendly guidelines against your recent topics before assuming the algorithm changed.</p>
<h2 id="a-realistic-path-to-the-threshold">A Realistic Path to the Threshold</h2>
<p>The numbers are achievable but not quick. A workable approach:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Pick a subject you can make 100 genuinely different videos about.</strong> If you can only think of 10 angles, the repetition policy will catch you by video 30.</li>
<li><strong>Publish consistently rather than in bursts.</strong> The watch-hour window is rolling; steady output keeps the total climbing.</li>
<li><strong>Optimise for watch time, not view count,</strong> on the long-form route. Twenty minutes watched from one viewer beats twenty one-minute views.</li>
<li><strong>Sort out verification and 2-Step early</strong> so nothing blocks you at the threshold.</li>
<li><strong>Read the monetization policies before you build a production pipeline,</strong> not after. Retrofitting variety into 50 template videos is much harder than designing for it.</li>
</ol>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-action">
<p class="callout-label">✅ Action Step</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>If you are building an AI-assisted channel, write down what makes each video materially different from the last — a distinct argument, original research, your own analysis, genuinely varied structure. If you can't answer that for a given video, it's the kind of content the policy is written to exclude.</p>
</div>
</div>
