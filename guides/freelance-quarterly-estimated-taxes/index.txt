<p>This guide describes rules the IRS publishes and applies to <strong>United States</strong> taxpayers. It is general information, not tax advice for your circumstances. Thresholds and dates change — confirm current details on the IRS pages linked below and speak to a professional about your own position.</p>

<p>An employer withholds tax from every payslip. A client doesn't. That difference is the entire reason estimated taxes exist, and it's why a freelancer's first tax year so often ends badly.</p>
<h2 id="the-threshold-that-triggers-it">The Threshold That Triggers It</h2>
<p>The IRS states individuals must pay estimated tax if they <strong>"expect to owe tax of $1,000 or more when their return is filed."</strong> For corporations the figure is <strong>$500 or more</strong>.</p>
<p>A thousand dollars of <em>tax owed</em> is not a thousand dollars of income. Between self-employment tax at 15.3% of net earnings and income tax on top, freelancers cross that line at a fairly modest level of earnings.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Note the wording: <strong>expect to owe</strong>. The obligation is forward-looking. You are meant to estimate at the start, not discover at the end — which is why it catches out people whose freelance income grew mid-year.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="the-safe-harbour">The Safe Harbour</h2>
<p>This is the most useful rule in the whole system, because it converts an unknowable number into an achievable target.</p>
<p>To avoid an underpayment penalty you generally need to pay at least:</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>90% of the tax for the current year, or 100% of the tax shown on the return for the prior year, whichever is smaller.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<p>The second option is the practical one. You already know last year's tax — it's on your return. Paying that amount across the year gives you a defined target, even if this year turns out much bigger.</p>
<p>The IRS notes that <strong>special rules apply to certain higher income taxpayers</strong>, with the detail in Publication 505. If your income is substantial, check that before relying on the prior-year figure.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-tip">
<p class="callout-label">💡 Pro Tip</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>For a freelancer whose income is growing, the prior-year safe harbour is usually the easier path. If you earned more this year, 100% of last year's smaller tax bill is a lower target than 90% of this year's larger one — and it still protects you from the penalty.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="a-refund-doesnt-save-you">A Refund Doesn't Save You</h2>
<p>The rule that surprises people most:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>"If you don't pay enough tax by the due date of each of the payment periods, you may be charged a penalty <strong>even if you are due a refund</strong> when you file your income tax return."</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The penalty is about <strong>when</strong> you paid, not whether you eventually overpaid. Someone who pays nothing all year and settles everything in April can still owe a penalty despite being square with the IRS overall.</p>
<h2 id="the-timing">The Timing</h2>
<p>The year is divided into <strong>four payment periods</strong>, each with a specific due date.</p>
<p>If a due date falls on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday, the IRS states the payment is on time if made on the next day that isn't one of those. For mailed payments, the US postmark date is the date of payment.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-warning">
<p class="callout-label">⚠️ Warning</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>This guide deliberately does not list the four dates. They shift for weekends and holidays and are set per tax year, and a stale date in an article is exactly the kind of error that causes a missed payment. Get the current year's dates from the IRS estimated taxes page or Form 1040-ES before you diarise them.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="you-dont-have-to-pay-quarterly">You Don't Have to Pay Quarterly</h2>
<p>A genuinely useful detail that the name obscures. The IRS states that if it is easier to pay <strong>weekly, bi-weekly or monthly</strong>, you can — as long as you have paid enough by the end of the quarter.</p>
<p>For freelancers with irregular income, paying a percentage of each invoice as it lands is far easier than finding a lump sum four times a year. The deadline is a checkpoint, not a required rhythm.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-pay">How to Pay</h2>
<p>The IRS lists several routes: Form 1040-ES by mail, online, by phone, through the IRS2Go app, or via your IRS online account, where you can also see your payment history.</p>
<h2 id="the-exception">The Exception</h2>
<p>You are not required to pay estimated tax if you <strong>had no tax liability for the prior year</strong> and meet two further conditions relating to citizenship or residency and having a 12-month tax year. Genuinely new freelancers in their first earning year should check whether this applies.</p>
<h2 id="a-routine-that-works">A Routine That Works</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Find last year's total tax</strong> from your return. That's your prior-year safe harbour target.</li>
<li><strong>Divide it across the year</strong> in whatever rhythm suits your cash flow.</li>
<li><strong>Move a percentage of every invoice</strong> into a separate account as it arrives, rather than trying to find money later.</li>
<li><strong>Check the current year's due dates</strong> on the IRS site and put them in your calendar.</li>
<li><strong>Reassess if income jumps sharply</strong>, since the higher-income rules may change what applies.</li>
</ol>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-action">
<p class="callout-label">✅ Action Step</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Look up the total tax on your last return today. That single number is your prior-year safe harbour target, and dividing it by twelve gives you a monthly amount that keeps you protected regardless of how this year turns out.</p>
</div>
</div>
