c:T2abf,<p>Before a site can earn anything from advertising, Google has to know it exists. New publishers routinely spend weeks tweaking content and layout while the real problem is much more basic: Googlebot either can't reach the pages, or has no reason to think they're worth crawling yet.</p>
<p>This guide covers what Google actually requires, in its own words, and what you can reasonably do to speed things along.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Indexing and ranking are different problems. Indexing means Google has your page in its database. Ranking means Google shows it to someone. This guide is about the first one — you cannot rank a page that was never indexed.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="the-three-technical-requirements">The Three Technical Requirements</h2>
<p>Google's documentation is unusually blunt here. There are exactly three minimum requirements for a page to be <em>eligible</em> for indexing:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Googlebot isn't blocked.</strong> Google must be able to find and access the page. Pages disallowed in <code>robots.txt</code>, or hidden behind a login, won't be indexed.</li>
<li><strong>The page works</strong> — meaning Google receives an <strong>HTTP 200 (success)</strong> status code. Client and server error pages aren't indexed.</li>
<li><strong>The page has indexable content</strong>, in a file type Google Search supports, that doesn't violate the spam policies.</li>
</ol>
<p>That's the whole list. Google is also explicit about what meeting it does <em>not</em> buy you: "just because a page meets these requirements doesn't mean that a page will be indexed; indexing isn't guaranteed."</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-warning">
<p class="callout-label">⚠️ Warning</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>The most common self-inflicted wound is shipping a site that was blocked during development. A <code>robots.txt</code> containing <code>Disallow: /</code> or a stray <code>noindex</code> meta tag left over from a staging environment will keep you invisible indefinitely, no matter how good the content is. Check both before you do anything else.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="check-youre-not-blocking-yourself">Check You're Not Blocking Yourself</h2>
<p>Two files decide whether Googlebot gets in.</p>
<p><strong><code>robots.txt</code></strong>, at the root of your domain. Visit <code>https://yourdomain.com/robots.txt</code> directly. You're looking for a <code>Disallow:</code> line that covers pages you want crawled. A healthy minimal file looks like:</p>
<pre><code>User-Agent: *
Allow: /

Sitemap: https://www.yourdomain.com/sitemap.xml
</code></pre>
<p><strong>The robots meta tag</strong>, in each page's HTML <code>&#x3C;head></code>. View source on a live page and search for <code>noindex</code>. If you find <code>&#x3C;meta name="robots" content="noindex"></code> on a page you want indexed, that single tag overrides everything else you do.</p>
<h2 id="build-a-sitemap-correctly">Build a Sitemap Correctly</h2>
<p>A sitemap is a machine-readable list of the URLs you want Google to know about. Most content management systems and site frameworks generate one automatically — check whether yours already serves <code>/sitemap.xml</code> before building anything by hand.</p>
<p>Google's stated limits for a single sitemap file are <strong>50MB uncompressed or 50,000 URLs</strong>. Beyond that you split into multiple sitemaps and reference them from a sitemap index. Almost no new site comes close to either limit.</p>
<p>The requirements that actually catch people out:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Use fully-qualified, absolute URLs</strong> — <code>https://www.example.com/mypage.html</code>, not <code>/mypage.html</code>.</li>
<li><strong>Files must be UTF-8 encoded.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Include only canonical URLs</strong> — the version of each page you actually want in search results. If your site is reachable at both <code>example.com/about</code> and <code>www.example.com/about/</code>, pick one, redirect the other, and list only the winner.</li>
<li><strong><code>lastmod</code> should reflect genuine content updates.</strong> Stamping every URL with today's date on every build teaches Google to ignore the field.</li>
</ul>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-tip">
<p class="callout-label">💡 Pro Tip</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>That third point is the one that quietly wastes crawl budget. Decide early whether your canonical URLs use <code>www</code> or not, and whether they end in a trailing slash — then make your sitemap, your canonical tags, and your redirects all agree. Mismatches here are invisible to visitors but very visible to Googlebot.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="submit-it-four-ways-one-that-matters">Submit It — Four Ways, One That Matters</h2>
<p>Google accepts sitemap submissions through:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Search Console</strong>, via the Sitemaps report — this is the one to use, because it's the only method that reports back whether Google could read the file and what errors it found.</li>
<li><strong>The Search Console API</strong>, for programmatic submission.</li>
<li><strong><code>robots.txt</code></strong>, by adding a <code>Sitemap:</code> line as shown above. Free, and worth doing regardless.</li>
<li><strong>WebSub</strong>, for Atom/RSS feeds.</li>
</ol>
<p>Do both 1 and 3. The <code>robots.txt</code> line means any crawler that finds your site discovers the sitemap; Search Console gives you the diagnostics.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-warning">
<p class="callout-label">⚠️ Warning</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Submitting a sitemap is a hint, not an instruction. Google's documentation says plainly that it "doesn't guarantee that Google will download the sitemap or use the sitemap for crawling." A sitemap helps Google find pages; it does not persuade Google that they deserve indexing.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="set-up-search-console-first-not-later">Set Up Search Console First, Not Later</h2>
<p>Search Console is free and is the only place Google tells you what it thinks of your site. Verify ownership by DNS record if you can — it covers the whole domain including subdomains, and survives redesigns.</p>
<p>Once verified, the two reports worth learning:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pages</strong> (the indexing report) tells you which URLs are indexed and, for those that aren't, the specific reason — "Discovered – currently not indexed", "Crawled – currently not indexed", "Excluded by 'noindex' tag", and so on. These labels are diagnostic gold; each one points at a different fix.</li>
<li><strong>URL Inspection</strong> lets you check a single URL's status and request indexing for it.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use "Request indexing" on your genuinely important pages when they're first published. Don't submit every page repeatedly — it doesn't accelerate anything and the quota is small.</p>
<h2 id="why-discovered-currently-not-indexed-is-normal-for-new-sites">Why "Discovered – Currently Not Indexed" Is Normal for New Sites</h2>
<p>This status confuses almost everyone. It means Google knows the URL exists but has chosen not to crawl it yet.</p>
<p>For a brand-new domain with no external links and no history, that's the expected state. Google allocates crawling effort based on signals it doesn't have for you yet. This is not a penalty and there's no button that fixes it.</p>
<p>What actually resolves it, in rough order of impact:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Time.</strong> New domains routinely take weeks to move from discovered to indexed.</li>
<li><strong>Genuine external links.</strong> A single link from a site Google already crawls frequently does more than any amount of resubmitting. This means real mentions — a relevant forum answer, a directory your business actually belongs in, a supplier or partner page — not purchased links, which are a spam policy violation.</li>
<li><strong>Internal linking.</strong> Pages linked from your homepage and from other articles get crawled sooner than orphans reachable only via the sitemap.</li>
<li><strong>Publishing consistently.</strong> A site that adds content regularly earns more frequent crawling than one that goes quiet after launch.</li>
</ul>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>"Crawled – currently not indexed" is a different and more serious message. It means Google fetched the page, looked at it, and decided not to index it. That's a content quality signal, and the fix is the page itself — not the technical setup.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="what-not-to-do">What Not to Do</h2>
<p>Google's spam policies describe the tactics that get pages ranked lower or removed entirely. The ones new publishers are most often tempted by:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Buying links</strong>, or joining link-exchange schemes, to manufacture the external links described above.</li>
<li><strong>Scaled content abuse</strong> — mass-producing pages, with or without AI assistance, primarily to rank rather than to help anyone.</li>
<li><strong>Site reputation abuse</strong> — hosting unrelated third-party content on your domain to borrow its ranking.</li>
<li><strong>Cloaking</strong> — showing Googlebot something different from what visitors see.</li>
<li><strong>Hidden text and keyword stuffing.</strong></li>
</ul>
<p>These are also the behaviours most likely to cost you AdSense approval later, since Google evaluates content quality for both.</p>

<p class="callout-label">✅ Action Step</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Do these four things today, in order: verify Search Console by DNS, confirm <code>robots.txt</code> isn't blocking you, check no page carries a stray <code>noindex</code>, and submit your sitemap. Then publish consistently and leave it alone for a few weeks — refreshing the indexing report daily changes nothing.</p>
