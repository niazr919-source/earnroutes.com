<h2 id="affiliate-four-requirements-all-small">Affiliate: Four Requirements, All Small</h2>
<p>Twitch states you must meet <strong>four</strong> requirements:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Requirement</th>
<th>Threshold</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Followers</td>
<td><strong>25</strong></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Total streamed</td>
<td><strong>4 hours</strong></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Different days streamed</td>
<td><strong>4</strong></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Average viewers</td>
<td><strong>3</strong> (across those 4 different days)</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>You must also enable <strong>Two-Factor Authentication</strong> before onboarding can begin — Twitch requires it for all Affiliates and Partners.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>An average of <strong>three</strong> viewers. Not three hundred. This is the genuinely accessible tier, and it is why "become a Twitch Affiliate" is achievable for almost anyone who streams consistently for a couple of weeks and tells a few friends.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Twitch also notes that streamers aged 13–17 may join with parental or guardian consent and supervision.</p>
<h2 id="partner-a-different-order-of-magnitude">Partner: A Different Order of Magnitude</h2>
<p>Twitch's Partnership criteria:</p>
<ul>
<li>Complete the <strong>Path to Partner</strong> achievement</li>
<li>For the <strong>two consecutive months</strong> before applying, have at least <strong>6 individual streams</strong> meeting a minimum of <strong>75 average viewers per 30-day period</strong></li>
<li>Broadcast content conforming to the <strong>Community Guidelines, Terms of Service and DMCA Guidelines</strong></li>
<li>Twitch also <strong>considers suspension history</strong> when evaluating applications</li>
</ul>
<p>Twitch adds that it may accept applications outside these criteria in certain circumstances, including exceptional viewership growth.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-warning">
<p class="callout-label">⚠️ Warning</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Note the jump: <strong>3 average viewers</strong> for Affiliate, <strong>75 average viewers</strong> for Partner — twenty-five times more, sustained over two consecutive months rather than four days. Anyone treating Affiliate as "nearly Partner" has badly misjudged the gap.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>One detail worth knowing before you plan around it: Twitch states that viewership from <strong>front-page promotions or embeds does not count</strong> toward viewership requirement calculations. A burst of borrowed attention will not carry you over the line.</p>
<h2 id="what-each-tier-actually-pays">What Each Tier Actually Pays</h2>
<p><strong>Bits.</strong> Twitch states it provides participating Partners a share of Bits revenue <strong>equal to 1 cent per Bit</strong> used to Cheer for them. So a viewer cheering 500 Bits is worth about $5 to the streamer.</p>
<p><strong>Subscriptions.</strong> Viewers subscribe at Tier 1, Tier 2, Tier 3, or free via Prime. Twitch notes that revenue from a subscription <strong>may vary depending on the country the subscriber lives in</strong>, since local subscription prices differ.</p>
<p><strong>Ads.</strong> Partners earn a share of revenue from ads on their channel.</p>
<p><strong>Emotes.</strong> Partners can unlock up to <strong>60</strong> channel emotes — not revenue directly, but a real retention tool.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Twitch does not state a single standard subscription split on its Partner overview page. What it does publish is the <strong>Plus Program</strong>, offering two levels of <em>enhanced</em> subscription net revenue share for qualifying Affiliates and Partners: <strong>Level 1 at 60/40</strong> and <strong>Level 2 at 70/30</strong>. Those being described as enhanced tells you the default sits below them.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="how-this-compares-to-youtube">How This Compares to YouTube</h2>
<p>Worth setting side by side, because creators often choose between them:</p>

<p>Twitch's entry bar is dramatically lower. YouTube's is higher but its published shares are clearer, and its content keeps earning long after upload — where a stream's value is mostly consumed live.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-tip">
<p class="callout-label">💡 Pro Tip</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>The two aren't mutually exclusive, and the sensible pattern is obvious once you see the thresholds: stream on Twitch where the monetization bar is low, and publish the edited highlights to YouTube where the content keeps working. One session, two channels, two revenue models.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="a-realistic-reading">A Realistic Reading</h2>
<p><strong>Affiliate is a milestone, not an income.</strong> Three average viewers cheering occasional Bits is not a living. What it does is switch monetization on, which matters because you cannot build subscriber habits on a channel that has no subscribe button.</p>
<p><strong>Partner is where the economics change</strong> — and 75 average viewers sustained for two months is a real audience, typically representing many months of consistent streaming.</p>
<p><strong>Live is unforgiving.</strong> Unlike a blog post or a YouTube video, a stream earns while it's happening and very little afterwards. That makes consistency of schedule matter far more than it does in other online income routes.</p>
<p><strong>Income is taxable.</strong> Bits, subs and ad revenue are self-employment income. In the US, self-employment tax alone is 15.3% of net earnings before income tax applies.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-action">
<p class="callout-label">✅ Action Step</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Check your Achievements Dashboard on Twitch rather than guessing where you stand — it tracks progress against all four Affiliate requirements directly. If you are close, the missing one is usually the 3-viewer average, which is a scheduling problem more than an audience problem.</p>
