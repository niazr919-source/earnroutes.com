c:T3072,<p>Getting rejected by Google AdSense is one of the most common — and most frustrating — early setbacks for new publishers, largely because the rejection emails are vague. "Low value content" or "site does not comply with policies" tells you almost nothing about what to actually fix.</p>
<p>This guide strips away the guesswork. It walks through, in order, exactly what Google evaluates during review, mapped to the specific pages, policies, and quality signals its published documentation and reviewers actually care about. I've taken more than a dozen sites through this process; the checklist below is the same one I use before clicking "Apply."</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>There is no secret trick to AdSense approval. Every item here maps to something Google states publicly in its eligibility requirements, Program policies, or Publisher policies. The work is real, but it's also finite and knowable.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="first-understand-what-google-is-actually-checking">First, Understand What Google Is Actually Checking</h2>
<p>AdSense approval is not a single automated test — it's a combination of automated checks and, for many applications, a human review. Google is essentially asking three questions:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Is this a real, functional website with genuine value?</strong> (Content and usability)</li>
<li><strong>Does it comply with the rules advertisers require?</strong> (Policy compliance)</li>
<li><strong>Is the person applying eligible and legitimate?</strong> (Account and ownership)</li>
</ol>
<p>Almost every rejection traces back to one of these three. Let's take them in the order Google effectively evaluates them.</p>
<h2 id="part-1-eligibility-basics-you-must-clear-first">Part 1: Eligibility Basics You Must Clear First</h2>
<p>Before content even matters, confirm you meet Google's baseline eligibility requirements:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>You must be at least 18 years old.</strong> If you're younger, the account must be in the name of a parent or guardian.</li>
<li><strong>You need your own domain.</strong> While AdSense technically allows some cases, in practice a self-owned domain (yourdomain.com) is strongly expected. Free subdomains signal a low-investment, temporary site.</li>
<li><strong>Your site must be live and fully accessible</strong> to Google's reviewers — no password walls, no "coming soon" placeholders, no broken navigation.</li>
<li><strong>The site must be primarily your own original content</strong>, in a supported language, and you must have full authority to place ads on it.</li>
</ul>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-warning">
<p class="callout-label">⚠️ Warning</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>If your Google account was previously associated with a banned AdSense account, a new application will almost certainly be rejected. Google ties bans to the person, not just the site. This is not something you can work around, and attempting to do so violates the Program policies.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="part-2-the-content-bar-where-most-rejections-happen">Part 2: The Content Bar — Where Most Rejections Happen</h2>
<p>"Low value content" is the single most common rejection reason, and it's really a cluster of related issues. Google's guidance on creating helpful, people-first content is the clearest map of what "high value" means. Work through each of these.</p>
<h3 id="publish-enough-genuinely-useful-content">Publish Enough Genuinely Useful Content</h3>
<p>There is no official minimum article count, but sites with only 3–5 thin posts are routinely rejected. Aim for <strong>15–25 substantive pages</strong> before applying, each one written to actually help a reader — not to hit a word count.</p>
<ul>
<li>Each article should thoroughly cover its topic. Depth beats volume: ten 1,500-word guides that answer a real question outperform forty 300-word posts.</li>
<li>Every page should have a clear reason to exist. Google's own self-assessment question is worth borrowing: <em>would this content be useful to a visitor who came to your site directly, not from a search engine?</em></li>
</ul>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-tip">
<p class="callout-label">💡 Pro Tip</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Read your own site as if you'd never seen it. If any page makes you think "there's not much here," a reviewer will think the same thing. Expand it or remove it before applying — a few weak pages can drag down the assessment of the whole site.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h3 id="make-it-unmistakably-original">Make It Unmistakably Original</h3>
<p>Google's Publisher policies prohibit monetizing content that is scraped, spun, or copied from other sources without adding value. This includes:</p>
<ul>
<li>Republished articles from other sites, even with attribution</li>
<li>AI-generated content published with no editorial review, original insight, or fact-checking</li>
<li>"Rewritten" content that just paraphrases a competitor's page</li>
</ul>
<p>Original doesn't mean you can't use tools to help you write — it means the final page must reflect genuine expertise, first-hand experience, or original synthesis that a reader couldn't get from simply reading the source you started from.</p>
<h3 id="cover-a-coherent-topic">Cover a Coherent Topic</h3>
<p>A site that's one part recipes, one part crypto tips, and one part song lyrics reads as a content farm. Pick a clear niche or a small set of related topics so the site has an identifiable purpose and audience.</p>
<h2 id="part-3-the-pages-google-expects-every-site-to-have">Part 3: The Pages Google Expects Every Site to Have</h2>
<p>This is the most fixable — and most commonly missed — category. Reviewers look for the pages that signal a legitimate, trustworthy operation. Publish all of these before applying:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>About page</strong> — who runs the site and why they're qualified to write about the topic. This is your E-E-A-T anchor; name real people and real credentials.</li>
<li><strong>Contact page</strong> — a working way to reach you (a form or a real email address). A site with no way to contact its owner looks anonymous and disposable.</li>
<li><strong>Privacy Policy</strong> — required. It must disclose how you use cookies and, specifically, that third-party vendors including Google use cookies to serve ads based on prior visits. This is not optional boilerplate; it's a Program-policy requirement for running AdSense.</li>
<li><strong>Terms of Service / Disclaimer</strong> — establishes the rules and limits of your content, and is expected for any site offering advice.</li>
</ul>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-action">
<p class="callout-label">✅ Action Step</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Before applying, click every item in your site's main navigation and footer. Every link should resolve to a real, complete page. Broken links, empty category pages, and "under construction" notices are concrete, checkable reasons for rejection — and among the easiest to fix.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="part-4-technical-and-usability-signals">Part 4: Technical and Usability Signals</h2>
<p>Google evaluates whether the site actually works well for the people advertisers want to reach.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mobile responsiveness is mandatory.</strong> The majority of traffic is mobile; a layout that breaks on a phone is a fast rejection. Test on a real device, not just a desktop browser window.</li>
<li><strong>Serve over HTTPS.</strong> A secure certificate is standard and free through most hosts; its absence is a red flag.</li>
<li><strong>Reasonable load speed.</strong> Bloated pages that take many seconds to load hurt both approval odds and, later, your revenue.</li>
<li><strong>Clean navigation.</strong> A visitor (and a reviewer) should be able to find their way around without dead ends.</li>
<li><strong>Get indexed first.</strong> Submit your sitemap in Google Search Console and confirm your key pages are indexed before applying. A site Google hasn't crawled is harder to evaluate.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="part-5-policy-compliance-the-non-negotiables">Part 5: Policy Compliance — The Non-Negotiables</h2>
<p>Some content categories will get you rejected regardless of quality, because advertisers won't run against them. Google's Publisher policies and content policies prohibit or restrict monetization of, among others:</p>
<ul>
<li>Adult or sexually explicit content</li>
<li>Content that promotes or facilitates dangerous or illegal acts</li>
<li>Hateful, harassing, or violent content</li>
<li>Content that infringes copyright (including reproducing others' work, and — explicitly — song lyrics you don't own the rights to)</li>
<li>Deceptive or misleading content, including fabricated claims</li>
</ul>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-warning">
<p class="callout-label">⚠️ Warning</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Even one page in a prohibited category can sink an entire application. If your site mixes compliant content with anything in these categories, remove the non-compliant pages before applying — don't assume a reviewer will overlook them because "most" of the site is fine.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="part-6-applying-and-placing-your-first-ads-correctly">Part 6: Applying — and Placing Your First Ads Correctly</h2>
<p>Once the checklist above is genuinely complete:</p>
<ol>
<li>Sign up at the official AdSense site and connect your domain.</li>
<li>Add the AdSense verification code to your site as instructed.</li>
<li>Submit for review. Approval can take anywhere from a few days to two weeks or more.</li>
</ol>
<p>When you're approved, how you place ads determines both your revenue and your continued good standing. Google's ad placement policies are strict about a few things:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Never place ads that encourage accidental clicks</strong> — for example, immediately beside a button or navigation element, or arranged to look like site content or a download link.</li>
<li><strong>Don't overload pages with ads.</strong> A wall of ads with little content violates the "no content, all ads" spirit of the policies and hurts the user experience.</li>
<li><strong>Never click your own ads</strong>, and never ask anyone else to. Invalid-click detection is aggressive, and violations can permanently disable your account with little recourse.</li>
</ul>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>AdSense pays out when your balance reaches the <strong>$100 payment threshold</strong>. New sites often take a couple of months of accumulated earnings to reach it, so don't be discouraged when your first month shows a small, un-paid balance — that's normal and expected.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="if-you-get-rejected-how-to-actually-fix-it">If You Get Rejected: How to Actually Fix It</h2>
<p>A rejection is not a ban — you can reapply after fixing the issues. When you get one:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Re-read the exact rejection reason.</strong> "Low value content" points to Part 2; a policy reason points to Part 5.</li>
<li><strong>Do a genuine audit, not a cosmetic one.</strong> Add real content, complete missing policy pages, and remove anything thin or non-compliant.</li>
<li><strong>Wait until changes are live and indexed</strong> before reapplying — applying again within hours with no real changes just earns another rejection.</li>
<li><strong>Fix the substance, not the surface.</strong> Most successful reapplications come from adding several strong, original articles and the missing About/Contact/Privacy pages — not from tweaking colors or moving a menu.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="realistic-expectations">Realistic Expectations</h2>
<p>A site that clears every item on this list — genuinely useful original content across 15–25 pages, complete policy pages, clean mobile usability, and no prohibited content — has a strong chance of approval, often on the first try. From there, early AdSense earnings for a small niche site typically land in the $100–$500/month range as traffic builds, scaling further as you grow toward the traffic levels that unlock higher-paying networks. The approval itself, though, is the gate — and it's one you clear with preparation, not
