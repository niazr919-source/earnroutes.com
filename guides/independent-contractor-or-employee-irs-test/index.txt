<div class="callout-body">
<p>This guide describes rules the IRS publishes, and applies to <strong>United States</strong> taxpayers. It is general information, not tax or legal advice about your situation. Classification disputes turn on specific facts — talk to a professional before acting on anything here.</p>
</div>

<p>Freelancers tend to assume the contract decides it. It says "independent contractor", so that's what you are.</p>
<p>The IRS does not work that way. It looks at how the relationship actually operates.</p>
<h2 id="the-standard-control">The Standard: Control</h2>
<p>The IRS applies <strong>common law rules</strong>, examining "the entire relationship" and considering "the extent of the right to direct and control the worker."</p>
<p>Note the phrase <strong>right to control</strong> — not whether control is exercised. A client who <em>could</em> dictate your hours and methods, but chooses not to, still counts under this test.</p>
<h2 id="the-three-categories-of-evidence">The Three Categories of Evidence</h2>
<p>The IRS groups the facts into three areas. Read each as a question about your actual working relationship.</p>
<p><strong>1. Behavioural control</strong></p>
<blockquote>
<p>"Does the company control or have the right to control what the worker does and how the worker does his or her job?"</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Being told <em>what</em> outcome to deliver is normal for contractors. Being told <em>how, when and in what order</em> to do it points toward employment.</p>
<p><strong>2. Financial control</strong></p>
<blockquote>
<p>"Are the business aspects of the worker's job controlled by the payer? (these include things like how worker is paid, whether expenses are reimbursed, who provides tools/supplies, etc.)"</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Contractors typically supply their own equipment, can make a profit or loss, carry unreimbursed expenses, and are free to offer services to the wider market.</p>
<p><strong>3. Type of relationship</strong></p>
<blockquote>
<p>"Are there written contracts or employee type benefits (that is, pension plan, insurance, vacation pay, etc.)? Will the relationship continue and is the work performed a key aspect of the business?"</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Two parts here catch people out. <strong>Permanence</strong> — an open-ended engagement looks more like employment than a defined project. And whether your work is <strong>a key aspect of the business</strong> — a developer contracting to a software company sits closer to the line than one contracting to a bakery.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-info">
<p class="callout-label">ℹ️ Good to Know</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>The contract appears in category three as <em>one item of evidence</em>, alongside benefits, permanence and how central the work is. It is a factor, not the answer. This is why "but my contract says contractor" is not the defence freelancers expect it to be.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="there-is-no-scorecard">There Is No Scorecard</h2>
<p>The single most useful sentence in the IRS guidance:</p>
<blockquote>
<p>"There is no 'magic' or set number of factors that 'makes' the worker an employee or an independent contractor and no one factor stands alone in making this determination."</p>
</blockquote>
<p>So the checklists circulating online that promise "if you meet 7 of these 12 tests you're a contractor" are inventing a structure the IRS explicitly says doesn't exist. The determination weighs the whole picture.</p>
<h2 id="when-its-genuinely-unclear-form-ss-8">When It's Genuinely Unclear: Form SS-8</h2>
<p>If classification is uncertain, either the business or the worker can file <strong>Form SS-8</strong>, and the IRS will review the facts and circumstances and issue an official determination.</p>
<p>The catch is timing. The IRS notes it "may take at least six months to receive a determination on your filing" — so this is a way to resolve an ongoing or structural question, not to settle something before next month's invoice.</p>
<h2 id="why-it-matters-to-you-not-just-the-client">Why It Matters to You, Not Just the Client</h2>
<p>Misclassification is usually framed as the <em>business's</em> risk, and it is: a business that treats an employee as a contractor without reasonable basis can be held liable for employment taxes for that worker. The IRS notes relief provisions exist where there is a reasonable basis and proper documentation.</p>
<p>But the consequences reach the worker too. Your classification determines whether you owe <strong>self-employment tax</strong> — 15.3% of net earnings, covering both halves of Social Security and Medicare — and whether anything is being withheld on your behalf at all.</p>
<p>A freelancer who assumed they were an employee, and discovers otherwise at filing time, faces a bill nobody set money aside for.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-tip">
<p class="callout-label">💡 Pro Tip</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>If you work for one client, on their schedule, with their equipment, indefinitely, doing work central to their business — the label on your contract is doing a lot of load-bearing work. That's the pattern worth raising with a professional, not ignoring.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2 id="what-to-actually-do">What to Actually Do</h2>
<p><strong>Look at how the relationship operates</strong>, not what it's called. Run the three categories against your biggest client honestly.</p>
<p><strong>Keep the evidence that supports contractor status</strong> if that's genuinely what you are: your own tools, multiple clients, defined projects, unreimbursed expenses, control over your own methods.</p>
<p><strong>Set money aside regardless.</strong> If you're a contractor, self-employment tax applies and nothing is withheld for you.</p>
<p><strong>Use SS-8 for structural uncertainty</strong>, understanding the six-month timeline.</p>
<div class="not-prose callout callout-action">
<p class="callout-label">✅ Action Step</p>
<div class="callout-body">
<p>Take your largest client and answer the three IRS questions in writing: who controls how the work is done, who controls the business aspects, and what the relationship looks like in terms of permanence and centrality. If the answers lean toward employment, that's a conversation to have with a professional now rather than at filing time.</p>
</div>
